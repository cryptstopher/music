Introduction

From the soundtrack to Teen Wolf (1985), played during the climactic basketball-game scene where Scott Howard finally chooses to play as himself instead of the wolf. Pure 80s pop optimism, big chord stabs, ringing major chords, a bridge that hammers the progression home like a victory lap. If "Life Fades Away" is funeral music, this is the opposite: a song built to make you believe everything is going to work out.

Key & Theory

The song lives in E major. The chord palette is small and almost entirely diatonic, six chords plus one borrowed surprise:

Chord Role Comment
E I tonic, the home base
F#m ii gentle pivot inside the chorus
G#m iii brief tension before C#m
A IV the lifting chord
B V dominant, pulls back to E
C#m vi relative minor, used heavily
C#maj7 borrowed one-time substitution in Chorus 2

The song's harmonic engine is the I, vi, IV, V loop, E, C#m, A, B, also known as the "doo-wop" or "50s" progression. Almost every section either runs the loop, a fragment of it, or a closely related variant. The bridge is literally the loop repeated four times in a row at double speed.

The one moment of harmonic surprise is C#maj7 in the second chorus, where C#m would be expected. Raising the minor 3rd of C# (E natural) to a major 3rd (E#) for one beat creates a brief shimmer before the line resolves through A and B back to E. Tiny detail, big lift.

Tips

  • LH plays the root in the bass; RH holds the triad
  • Most chords have at least one black key. Use 1, 3, 5 on root-position triads even when the bottom note is a black key (C#, F#, G#), the hand drops naturally onto the keys
  • E to C#m and C#m to A are voice-leading cousins. Going E to C#m, only one note moves (B drops to C#). Going C#m to A, only one note moves (G# drops to A). Three-chord motion, two single-note shifts, very smooth
  • The chorus runs G#m to C#m. This is iii to vi, a jazz/pop turnaround that adds harmonic depth at the end of the chorus phrase. Voice it carefully: G#m (G# B D#) shares the G# with C#m (C# E G#), so keep G# as a common tone in the right hand and let the bass do the work
  • C#maj7 appears once, in Chorus 2, where C#m normally sits. See below

The C#maj7 Substitution

The chord C#maj7 is built C# E# G# B# (which on the piano is C# F G# C). It is one half-step away from C#m7 (C# E G# B), the only difference is E natural becomes E# (F) and B natural becomes B# (C). 

C#m7:    C#, E,  G#, B    (the diatonic version)
C#maj7:  C#, E#, G#, B#   (the borrowed version)

In E major, the natural chord at C# is C#m, the relative minor (vi). Substituting C#maj7 turns vi into a major-7 chord and introduces two notes (E# and B#) that are foreign to the key. The effect is a momentary brightening, the line "Isn't that the way you do it?" suddenly lifts, and then settles back when A follows. 

LH: C# (bass)
RH: E#, G#, B#    (3rd, 5th, 7th)
Fingering: 1, 2, 3, 5

This is sometimes called "borrowing from the parallel major", treating the C# of C#m as a major-key tonic for one beat. It is a small move with a big harmonic punch. Use it only on the second chorus, exactly where the chart calls for it. Plain C#m on every other repeat keeps the surprise fresh when the substitution finally arrives.

Inversions for Voice Leading

The chorus moves B, E, A, F#m, B in close succession, which means the bass jumps around: B, E, A, F#, B. Smoothing this with inversions tightens the right hand and keeps the line singing.

Voice-leading tip:

The most common chorus phrase is A, F#m, B, E. Try this bass line: A, A (F#m/A), B, E. Pedal the A through both A and F#m chords by using F#m's first inversion, the bass holds steady, only the inner voices shift.

Performance Notes

  • Tempo: bright and bouncy, around 130 BPM. This is a victory song, do not drag it
  • Touch: full and ringing. Major chords with the sustain pedal down, let them shine
  • Pedal: change on every chord, no smearing. The chord changes are the whole texture
  • The Bridge: E, C#m, A, B played four times in rapid succession. Make sure the bass clearly walks E, C#, A, B each time, the listener should hear the descent
  • The C#maj7: hold this chord one beat longer than the surrounding chords. The substitution only works if it is heard. Lean into it
  • The Outro: the chart shows a dense single-note melodic line, this is the lead guitar riff over a sustained progression. On piano, you have two options: (1) play the riff as a single-note melody in the right hand while the left hand holds whole-bar chord roots, or (2) skip the riff and just play the implied chord progression E, A, B, E as ringing chords until the song fades. Both work

Bridge note:

The opening of the Bridge runs the I, vi, IV, V loop four times in rapid succession (the chart shows E, C#m, A, B repeated, with the last cell ending on C#m, A as a half-cadence into the lyric). Play these as quick chord stabs, one chord per beat, no sustain pedal. The bass walks E, C#, A, B over and over, which gives the section its drive. When the lyric enters on C#m, drop into a slower, fuller texture.

Outro note:

The chart shows a dense melodic line, single notes (or close-position chord stabs) cycling through the same diatonic palette. This is the lead-guitar fade-out, not really a chord progression you would play as a piano part. Three options for solo piano:

  1. Play it as a melodic line. Treat each label as a single note in the right hand: E, G#, E, F#, G#, E, F#, B, G#, G#, etc. Hold a sustained E in the left hand throughout
  2. Reduce it to a chord vamp. Play E, A, B, E or E, C#m, A, B as ringing chords with the sustain pedal, ignoring the melodic detail
  3. Mix. Hold a chord in the left hand and improvise a melody on top using the E major scale

The song fades out, so there is no defined end, just keep the energy up until you fade your own volume to zero.
