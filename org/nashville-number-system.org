#+TITLE: The Nashville Number System
#+SUBTITLE: A Practical Guide for Song Composition
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:t

* What Is the Nashville Number System?

The Nashville Number System (NNS) is a shorthand method of writing chord
charts using numbers instead of chord letter names. Each number represents
a scale degree of the major scale in whatever key you're playing in.

It was developed by studio musicians in Nashville who needed to transpose
songs on the fly during recording sessions. Instead of rewriting a chart
in every new key, a number chart works in *all* keys---you just announce
the key and play.

* The Basics

** Scale Degrees

In any major key, the seven notes of the scale each get a number:

| Degree | 1    | 2     | 3     | 4    | 5    | 6     | 7         |
|--------+------+-------+-------+------+------+-------+-----------|
| Role   | I    | ii    | iii   | IV   | V    | vi    | vii°      |
| Quality| Major| minor | minor | Major| Major| minor | diminished|

- *1, 4, 5* are major chords.
- *2, 3, 6* are minor chords.
- *7* is diminished (rarely used as-is in pop/country).

** How to Read a Number

- A plain number (e.g. =1=) means a major chord built on that scale degree.
- A dash after a number (e.g. =2-=) means minor. Some writers use a
  lowercase =m= instead: =2m=.
- A superscript or caret for seventh chords: =5^7= or =57= means a
  dominant 7th on the 5.

Because the default chord qualities of the major scale are so well known,
many charts omit the minor symbol on 2, 3, and 6 entirely---it is implied.
If a chord *deviates* from the expected quality, it is always marked
(e.g. =4-= for a minor 4 chord, or =2= with no dash to indicate a major 2).

* Notation Conventions

** Rhythm and Duration

| Symbol       | Meaning                                          |
|--------------+--------------------------------------------------|
| =1  4=       | Each number gets equal time (usually one bar)    |
| =1  4  5  1= | Four bars, one chord per bar                     |
| =1 . 4 .=    | Dots split a bar: two beats on 1, two beats on 4 |
| =1 . . 4=    | Three beats on 1, one beat on 4                  |
| =<>=         | Diamond---hit and let ring (whole note feel)     |
| =>=          | Push---play the chord an eighth note early       |

** Repeated Sections

- =%= :: Repeat the previous bar.
- Draw a box or bracket around a section and write =x3= to repeat 3 times.

** Other Markings

| Symbol            | Meaning                                   |
|-------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| =#=               | Sharp the root (e.g. =#4= = raised 4th)   |
| =b=               | Flat the root (e.g. =b7= = flatted 7th)   |
| =sus=             | Suspended 4th (e.g. =1sus=)               |
| =add9=            | Add the 9th (e.g. =1add9=)                |
| =2-7=             | Minor 7th chord on the 2                  |
| =5/7=             | 5 chord with the 7th scale degree in bass |
| =/5= (standalone) | Just a bass note (5) with no chord above  |

* Common Progressions

** Pop / Rock Staples

*** 1 - 5 - 6- - 4 (The "Axis" Progression)

The most ubiquitous progression in modern pop music.

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 5     | 6-    | 4     |

- Key of G: G - D - Em - C
- Key of C: C - G - Am - F
- Songs: "Let It Be," "No Woman No Cry," "With or Without You"

*** 1 - 4 - 5 - 4

A driving, anthemic rock progression.

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 4     | 5     | 4     |

- Key of A: A - D - E - D
- Key of E: E - A - B - A
- Songs: "Louie Louie," "Wild Thing"

** Country / Folk

*** 1 - 4 - 1 - 5

The backbone of countless country and folk songs.

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 4     | 1     | 5     |

- Key of G: G - C - G - D
- Key of D: D - G - D - A
- Songs: "Jambalaya," "Ring of Fire" (verse)

*** 1 - 4 - 5 - 1 (Basic Three-Chord)

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 4     | 5     | 1     |

- Key of E: E - A - B - E
- Key of C: C - F - G - C
- Songs: "Twist and Shout," "La Bamba"

** Singer-Songwriter / Emotional

*** 1 - 6- - 4 - 5

Classic doo-wop and ballad progression.

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 6-    | 4     | 5     |

- Key of C: C - Am - F - G
- Key of G: G - Em - C - D
- Songs: "Stand By Me," "Every Breath You Take"

*** 6- - 4 - 1 - 5

Same chords, starting on the minor. Creates a darker, more urgent feel.

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 6-    | 4     | 1     | 5     |

- Key of C: Am - F - C - G
- Key of E: C#m - A - E - B
- Songs: "Save Tonight," "Numb"

** Blues

*** 12-Bar Blues: 1 - 1 - 1 - 1 | 4 - 4 - 1 - 1 | 5 - 4 - 1 - 5

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 1     | 1     | 1     |
| 4     | 4     | 1     | 1     |
| 5     | 4     | 1     | 5     |

With dominant 7ths (standard blues feel): use =17=, =47=, =57= throughout.

- Key of E: E7 - E7 - E7 - E7 | A7 - A7 - E7 - E7 | B7 - A7 - E7 - B7
- Key of A: A7 - A7 - A7 - A7 | D7 - D7 - A7 - A7 | E7 - D7 - A7 - E7

** Progressions Using Non-Diatonic Chords

*** 1 - b7 - 4 - 1 (Mixolydian / "Hey Jude" Ending)

The b7 is borrowed from the parallel minor (mixolydian mode). Written
with a flat sign to show it is *not* the diatonic 7dim.

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | b7    | 4     | 1     |

- Key of C: C - Bb - F - C
- Key of D: D - C - G - D

*** 1 - 4 - b6 - b7 (Borrowed Chords)

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | 4     | b6    | b7    |

- Key of C: C - F - Ab - Bb
- Key of G: G - C - Eb - F

* Parallel Minor and Borrowed Chords

** What Is a Parallel Minor?

Every major key has a *parallel minor*---a minor key that shares the same
root note. For example, C major and C minor are parallel keys. They start
on the same note but use different scales, which produces a different set
of chord qualities.

In the Nashville Number System, the major scale is the default frame of
reference. When you "borrow" a chord from the parallel minor, you are
temporarily using a chord that belongs to the minor scale built on the
same root. These borrowed chords are marked with flats (=b3=, =b6=, =b7=)
to distinguish them from the diatonic major-scale chords.

** Comparing Major and Parallel Minor Chords

The following table shows the diatonic chords for a major key and its
parallel natural minor side by side:

| Degree | Major Key     | Parallel Minor  |
|--------+---------------+-----------------|
|      1 | 1 (Major)     | 1- (minor)      |
|      2 | 2- (minor)    | 2dim (diminished)|
|     b3 | ---           | b3 (Major)      |
|      3 | 3- (minor)    | ---             |
|      4 | 4 (Major)     | 4- (minor)      |
|      5 | 5 (Major)     | 5- (minor)      |
|     b6 | ---           | b6 (Major)      |
|      6 | 6- (minor)    | ---             |
|     b7 | ---           | b7 (Major)      |
|      7 | 7dim (diminished) | ---         |

In the key of C, for example:

| Degree | Major Key | Parallel Minor |
|--------+-----------+----------------|
|      1 | C         | Cm             |
|      2 | Dm        | Ddim           |
|   b3/3 | Em        | Eb             |
|      4 | F         | Fm             |
|      5 | G         | Gm             |
|   b6/6 | Am        | Ab             |
|   b7/7 | Bdim      | Bb             |

** Most Commonly Borrowed Chords

Not all parallel minor chords are borrowed equally. In rough order of
frequency in pop, rock, and country music:

- *b7* (e.g. Bb in the key of C) :: The single most common borrowed chord.
  Gives a grounded, mixolydian flavor. Found in countless rock outros and
  verse turnarounds.

- *b6* (e.g. Ab in the key of C) :: Creates a dramatic, cinematic quality.
  Often paired with b7 in a plagal-style cadence: =b6 - b7 - 1=.

- *b3* (e.g. Eb in the key of C) :: Adds a bluesy, earthy sound. Common
  in classic rock and singer-songwriter music.

- *4-* (minor 4, e.g. Fm in the key of C) :: The iconic "minor four." Its
  bittersweet quality is a favorite for emotional pre-choruses and
  bridges. Written =4-= in NNS.

- *5-* (minor 5, e.g. Gm in the key of C) :: Less common but
  distinctive. Often used in passing or as a surprise substitution.

** Notation in NNS

Borrowed chords are written with accidentals to signal that they deviate
from the major-scale default:

| NNS Symbol | Meaning                               | In Key of G |
|------------+---------------------------------------+-------------|
| =b7=       | Major chord on the flatted 7th degree | F           |
| =b6=       | Major chord on the flatted 6th degree | Eb          |
| =b3=       | Major chord on the flatted 3rd degree | Bb          |
| =4-=       | Minor chord on the 4th degree         | Cm          |
| =5-=       | Minor chord on the 5th degree         | Dm          |

If a borrowed chord needs a seventh, write it as you would any other:
=b77= or =b7^7= for a dominant 7th on the b7, =4-7= for a minor 7th on
the minor 4, and so on.

** Example Progressions Using Parallel Minor Borrowing

*** 1 - b3 - 4 - 1 (Rock Borrowing)

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | b3    | 4     | 1     |

- Key of A: A - C - D - A
- Key of E: E - G - A - E
- Songs: "Sympathy for the Devil" (verse vamp)

*** 1 - 4- - 1 (The Minor Four Resolution)

| Bar 1   | Bar 2 | Bar 3 |
|---------+-------+-------|
| 1       | 4-    | 1     |

- Key of C: C - Fm - C
- Key of D: D - Gm - D
- Songs: "Creep" (Radiohead, uses 4- in the progression), "Space Oddity"

*** 1 - b6 - b7 - 1 (Aeolian Cadence)

| Bar 1 | Bar 2 | Bar 3 | Bar 4 |
|-------+-------+-------+-------|
| 1     | b6    | b7    | 1     |

- Key of G: G - Eb - F - G
- Key of C: C - Ab - Bb - C
- Songs: "Stairway to Heaven" (ending section feel)

** Why Borrowing Works

Borrowed chords work because they share the same tonal center as the
major key. The root note (1) stays "home," so the listener still feels
grounded even when a foreign chord appears. The ear interprets borrowed
chords as colorful *inflections* rather than key changes, which is why
they can add emotional weight without sounding jarring.

A practical rule of thumb: if a chord has the same root as one of your
diatonic chords but a different quality (major instead of minor or vice
versa), it is likely a borrowed chord from the parallel minor. In NNS,
just mark it with the appropriate accidental or quality change and any
musician will know what to do.

* Writing a Chart: Putting It All Together

Here is an example chart for a simple song in the style of a Nashville
number chart:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Title: Example Song
Key: G
Tempo: 120 BPM
Time: 4/4

INTRO:
| 1     | 4     |

VERSE:
| 1     | 4     | 1     | 5     |
| 1     | 4     | 5     | 1     |

CHORUS:
| 4     | 1     | 5     | 6-    |
| 4     | 1     | 5     | 1     |

BRIDGE:
| 4     | 4     | 1     | 1     |
| 2-    | 5     | <5>   |

TAG:
| 1     |
#+END_EXAMPLE

Reading this chart:
- The =<5>= in the bridge means hit the 5 chord and let it ring (diamond).
- Any musician can play this in the key of G, or the leader can call a
  new key and the chart works without any changes.

* Quick Reference: Transposing with Numbers

To transpose, simply change the key and look up new letter names in the
reference table. The numbers never change.

*Example:* Move =1 - 5 - 6- - 4= from the key of G to the key of Bb.

| Number | Key of G | Key of Bb |
|--------+----------+-----------|
|      1 | G        | Bb        |
|      5 | D        | F         |
|     6- | Em       | Gm        |
|      4 | C        | Eb        |

That's the entire point of the system---learn the numbers once, play in
any key forever.

* Tips for Composition

1. *Start with a feel, not a key.* Write your progression as numbers
   first, then pick the key that suits the singer's range.

2. *Borrow from parallel minor.* Using b3, b6, and b7 chords adds color
   without leaving the tonal center. These are the most common
   non-diatonic chords in pop music.

3. *Use the 5 chord to create tension.* The 5 (and especially 57) wants
   to resolve to 1. Place it before your tonic for a strong resolution.

4. *Swap 5 for 5sus.* A =5sus= resolving to =5= then =1= adds drama
   and is a staple of worship and anthemic music.

5. *Try starting on a chord other than 1.* Beginning on 4 or 6- can
   create immediate emotional interest before "arriving home" at 1.

6. *Secondary dominants.* Writing =5/5= (the 5 of 5) or =5/2= gives
   you strong pull toward chords other than the tonic. In the key of C,
   =5/5= is D major (which pulls toward G).

7. *Keep it simple.* Many of the greatest songs ever written use only
   three or four chords. The Nashville Number System makes it obvious
   just how much mileage you can get from 1, 4, 5, and 6-.
