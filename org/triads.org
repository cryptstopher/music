#+SUBTITLE: Learning the triad for each scale degree in all 12 keys

* What Is a Triad?

A triad is a chord built from three notes stacked in thirds---root, third, and fifth.
That's it.
Every chord you play in a major key starts as a triad.

The *root* is the note the chord is named after.
The *third* determines whether the chord feels major or minor.
The *fifth* stabilizes the sound.

* The Four Triad Types

In this table, the "R" stands for root, "M" stands for major, "m" stands for minor, and "P" stands for perfect.
The scale degree from I to V is a perfect fifth?

| Type        | Formula            | Interval Stack      | Example (root C) |
|-------------+--------------------+---------------------+------------------|
| Major       | R + M3 + P5        | 4 semitones, then 3 | C  E  G          |
| minor       | R + m3 + P5        | 3 semitones, then 4 | C  Eb G          |
| diminished  | R + m3 + d5        | 3 semitones, then 3 | C  Eb Gb         |
| augmented   | R + M3 + A5        | 4 semitones, then 4 | C  E  G#         |

- *Major* sounds bright and stable.
- *minor* sounds darker, but still stable.
- *diminished* sounds tense and unstable — it wants to resolve.
- *augmented* is rare in diatonic harmony; you'll mostly encounter it in chromatic passages.

* Triads in a Major Key

Every major key produces the same pattern of triad types across its seven scale degrees.
Learn this pattern once and it applies to all 12 keys.

| Degree | Roman | Quality    | Formula reminder   |
|--------+-------+------------+--------------------|
|      1 | I     | Major      | R M3 P5            |
|      2 | ii    | minor      | R m3 P5            |
|      3 | iii   | minor      | R m3 P5            |
|      4 | IV    | Major      | R M3 P5            |
|      5 | V     | Major      | R M3 P5            |
|      6 | vi    | minor      | R m3 P5            |
|      7 | vii°  | diminished | R m3 d5            |

The pattern is: *Major minor minor Major Major minor diminished.*
Say it until it sticks---it unlocks every key at once.

- Degrees 1, 4, and 5 are always major.
- Degrees 2, 3, and 6 are always minor.
- Degree 7 is always diminished.

* How Semitone Distances Work Between Degrees

Music counts intervals *inclusively* — it includes the note you start on.
A "third" means you count C(1) D(2) E(3): three note names, but only two steps of motion.
This makes every interval name one higher than its arithmetic distance.

** The Major Scale Step Pattern

A major scale is not evenly spaced. It follows a fixed pattern of whole steps (W = 2 semitones)
and half steps (H = 1 semitone):

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
C   D   E   F   G   A   B   C
  W   W   H   W   W   W   H
  2   2   1   2   2   2   1   semitones per step
#+END_EXAMPLE

The half steps fall at iii→IV (E to F) and vii→I (B to C).
Everything else is a whole step.
That uneven spacing is why the semitone distances between degrees are not uniform.

** Semitones From I to Each Degree (in C major)

| Degree | Roman | Note | Semitones from I | Interval Name |
|--------+-------+------+------------------+---------------|
|      1 | I     | C    |                0 | Unison        |
|      2 | ii    | D    |                2 | Major 2nd     |
|      3 | iii   | E    |                4 | Major 3rd     |
|      4 | IV    | F    |                5 | Perfect 4th   |
|      5 | V     | G    |                7 | Perfect 5th   |
|      6 | vi    | A    |                9 | Major 6th     |
|      7 | vii°  | B    |               11 | Major 7th     |

- The Roman numeral tells you the *interval name* (4th, 5th…), not the semitone count.
- To find the semitone distance: count every piano key — white and black — between the two roots.
- The chord quality (major or minor) does not change the root's semitone distance.
  I to IV and I to iv both have the root F five semitones above C.

** "Major interval" vs "minor chord" — two different uses of the same word

The word major/minor appears in two separate systems and means something different in each.

- *Interval quality* describes the size of the gap between two notes.
- *Chord quality* describes the internal structure of a chord — whether its third is raised or lowered.

When you measure from the tonic of a major scale up to any note within that scale,
every interval is either major or perfect — never minor.
That is not a coincidence: it is the definition of a major scale.

| From C to | Semitones | Interval name | Chord at that degree |
|-----------+-----------+---------------+----------------------|
| D         |         2 | Major 2nd     | Dm — a minor chord   |
| E         |         4 | Major 3rd     | Em — a minor chord   |
| A         |         9 | Major 6th     | Am — a minor chord   |

The chord built on D is minor because its internal third (D to F) is a minor third (3 semitones).
That has nothing to do with how far D sits above C.
Lowering D to Db would give you a minor 2nd from C — but Db is not in C major.

* Root Position and Inversions

A triad can be played three ways — same three notes, different note on the bottom.

| Name           | Bottom Note | Example (C major) | Use                            |
|----------------+-------------+-------------------+--------------------------------|
| Root position  | Root        | C E G             | Clearest, most grounded        |
| First inversion| Third       | E G C             | Smooth bass movement           |
| Second inversion| Fifth      | G C E             | Passing chord, cadential 6/4   |

- Root position is your default. Learn all 12 major I chords in root position first.
- First inversion is how you connect chords without jumping around in the bass.
- Second inversion sounds less stable — use it deliberately, not as a default.

The notes are always the same. Only the voicing changes.
